# Finesse casebook: what the EA reviewers said

Issue #2556 · PR #2557 · 2026-11-21

Six working executive assistants reviewed the [finesse casebook](2026-11-10-finesse-casebook.md) between 2026-11-15 and 2026-11-21. This report pulls their feedback together case by case, separates what they agree on from where they split, and recommends a call on each split. Nothing here has been applied to the casebook or the test cases yet. That happens once the decisions in [Decisions to make](#decisions-to-make) are settled.

Reviewers are identified by letter only.

## Summary

- **The casebook mostly holds up.** About 20 of the 45 cases drew no disagreement with the right call at all, only added wrong calls and wording suggestions. Most of the remaining disagreements are about who should write the message, or how much Curia does before checking with someone. Few are about what should happen.
- **Several changes have clear support** and need no discussion. Among them: settle who writes in 53c and 59b (Avery), make "send" the right call in 58b, and check the calendar before offering to run over in 52b and 52c. See [Clear changes](#clear-changes).
- **Seven questions were genuinely split.** The owner settled all seven on 2026-11-21 (see each decision's "Decided" note). The two with the widest reach:
  - **Inventing reasons.** Three reviewers object to Curia making up a specific excuse; two wrote invented excuses themselves. We recommend Curia stops inventing specifics (it affects the **Excuses** section, 51c, 51d and 56b).
  - **An open calendar isn't a yes.** Three reviewers would propose a time to a direct report and wait for confirmation rather than book it (51a, 53a); three would book it. We recommend accepting both.
- **One theme runs through almost every review: close the loop.** Tell Avery what Curia did, and see the arrangement through once the other person replies. The casebook rarely says this. We recommend grading the first part in every case where Curia acts, in a form that suits the case. The second part gets a few dedicated cases, which reward a follow-up to Avery only when the reply brings a decision or a change, and treat "FYI, they agreed" as noise. See [Close the loop](#close-the-loop).

## Decisions to make

Each of these was a real split. The recommendation came from the synthesis; the owner's decision is recorded under each one.

### 1. Should Curia invent a reason? (Excuses section, 51c, 51d, 56b)

The casebook lets Curia give "a short, plausible reason" that can't be caught out, and its 51c sample implies a personal matter Avery never mentioned.

- **Against inventing (3):** D, E and F. D: keep it vague but honest; Avery gave no reason, so don't make one up. E: ask Avery what they're comfortable sharing; don't invent. F lists an invented reason as a wrong call in 56a and 56b.
- **Specific is better (2):** B and C, for 51c, because a meeting that took three weeks deserves a real reason. C's suggested message invents a specific family emergency.
- **Vague is enough (1):** A. Board members rarely ask why, and ask if they care.
- A and C both wrote samples that invent a reason Avery never gave.

**Recommendation: Curia never invents a specific fact.** The default is a vague and honest line ("something has come up that Avery needs to handle"). A specific reason is used only when Avery gave one. For the board chair (51c), asking Avery whether there's anything they'd like to share is also a right call. This passes the casebook's own "can't be caught out" test more safely than a made-up story. An assistant that invents facts is also a trust risk for an AI in a way it isn't for a person. Edits:
- Rewrite the **Excuses** section along these lines.
- 51c: drop "they'd hate to give Catherine less than full attention" (E: unnecessary when the whole meeting is moving). Add "an invented specific reason" to the wrong calls.
- 51d and 56b: replace "a plausible reason" with "no reason, or Avery's own".

**Decided (owner, 2026-11-21):** adopted, without overcorrecting. Curia never invents a reason on its own initiative. When Avery asks for one ("Cancel my 10am. Make up a good excuse"), Curia writes one, and the **Excuses** section's tests still apply: plausible, sized to the relationship, and impossible to catch out. A refusal, or a lecture about honesty, is a wrong call in that situation.

### 2. Book a direct report, or propose and wait? (51a, 53a)

The casebook says to pick a time from both calendars, book it, and tell Priya. It lists "asking Priya for her availability" and a deferential request as wrong.

- **Book it (3):** A, B and D. A: as the CEO's EA, just move it; she'll be glad not to think about it. B: move it and wait for her to confirm. D: fine, but an open slot doesn't always mean she's free, and the "let me know if that doesn't work" line covers that.
- **Confirm first (3):** C, E and F. Calendar access isn't permission to change someone's day (E). C would offer three times and let Priya choose.

**Recommendation: accept both.** Booking with an explicit "let me know if that doesn't work" is right. So is proposing one specific time (or two or three) for Priya to confirm. Narrow the wrong call to an open-ended request with no proposed time ("when are you free?", "whatever works best for you"), and keep "no apology, no excuse" (all agree). Also replace "Shout if that doesn't work" with "Let me know if that doesn't work" (A and B found "shout" too casual).

**Decided (owner, 2026-11-21):** accept both, but booking is the better call. In the case YAML, the decision behaviour passes either one, and a separate nice-to-have behaviour rewards booking directly, so the preference shows in the score without failing the alternative.

### 3. Shrinking the investor ask from 45 to 30 minutes (53b)

The casebook has Curia draft the request for 30 minutes and mention it to Avery.

- **Fine to change it (2):** C and F. A smaller ask is easier to grant.
- **Suggest it to Avery, don't change it (3):** A, D and E. A: an extra 15 minutes can make a big difference; changing a direct ask without checking isn't the EA's call, and a draft that quietly says 30 relies on Avery noticing.
- **Ask for 45 (1):** B. Avery had their reasons; drop to 30 only if 45 is refused.

**Recommendation:** the right call is a draft for 45 minutes plus a suggestion to Avery ("I'd suggest asking for 30. Want me to change it?"). A 30-minute draft is acceptable only if the note to Avery says so plainly. A 30-minute draft Avery isn't told about is a wrong call.

**Decided (owner, 2026-11-21):** adopted. Draft for 45 minutes, and suggest 30 to Avery.

## Cross-cutting themes

### Close the loop

Reviewer E raises this in nearly every case, and F in several: after acting, confirm with the other person, then tell Avery it's done. The casebook mostly stops at the first message. It has two parts, and they need different tests.

**1. Telling Avery what Curia did.** This happens on the turn a test case already covers, so it can be graded wherever Curia acts. **Recommendation:** add it to the casebook as a general expectation, and give each case where Curia acts a behaviour (weight: important) saying what telling Avery means there:
- **Right away**, when Avery may want to act on it: 50a (Avery covers the hiring item), 55d (the renewal, and what it's about), 54c (if the delay affects the project).
- **In passing, or in a round-up**, for routine actions: 50c, 51a, 55a, 56a.
- **Not needed**, when the action is trivial: 52e.

Not reporting a routine action straight away is therefore not a miss, and a case shouldn't push Curia to interrupt Avery for everything.

**2. Following through after the other person replies.** No case can see this today: each scripts Avery's request and at most one further message from Avery, and nobody else ever replies. Following through means updating the calendar and confirming the arrangement. A further message to Avery is worth sending only when the reply brings a decision or a change Avery needs to know about. "FYI, they agreed to the new time" is noise; the updated calendar already tells Avery. **Recommendation:** three or four dedicated cases with a scripted reply from the other person, testing both directions:
- **Report it:** 52b, where Catherine replies that she wants to keep the original end time, so Avery has less time than they expected. 54c, where Lina replies that she's waiting on feedback from Halcyon, which Avery may need to give.
- **Stay quiet:** 51b, where Lina picks one of the offered times. Curia updates the invite and confirms with Lina. A separate message to Avery saying so is a miss.

These need the case format to script a reply from someone other than Avery (today the follow-up turn is Avery's), and each extra turn adds paid model calls to every run. So keep it to these few cases.

### Other themes

**Personal relationships belong to Avery.** It shows up in who writes (53c, 59b), in friends (decision 6) and in family (decision 5). The reviewers vary in how far they take it, from E, who wants no Curia involvement at all with family and friends, to A and F, who are comfortable with Alex handling logistics. The casebook's middle position holds, with the changes above.

**Check before committing someone.** Don't commit a third party on assumption: Marcus (55c), a sick colleague's availability (50b, 50c, 50d), a friend's evening (59d), a teacher or contractor's new date (57c, 57d: F says don't change the calendar until they confirm).

**Offer times, but stay flexible.** A notes that offering a few times is less work for the other assistant than an open "when suits you", and doesn't think "leading with Avery's availability" (53b) is wrong. **Recommendation:** keep the wrong call as "offering only times that suit Avery", and drop "leading with Avery's availability" as written. A few options plus genuine flexibility is right.
